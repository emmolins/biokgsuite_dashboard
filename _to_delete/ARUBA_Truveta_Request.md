# Data Request: Target Trial Emulation of ARUBA

**Requested by:** Emily Molins (DPhil)  ·  **Audience:** Truveta Data Engineering
**Purpose:** To define the patient cohort and data elements needed to emulate the ARUBA randomized trial in real-world electronic health record (EHR) data.

---

## Overview

This request concerns a study of **brain arteriovenous malformations (AVMs)** — tangles of abnormal blood vessels in which arteries connect directly to veins, bypassing the normal capillary bed. An AVM can rupture and cause a haemorrhagic stroke; an *unruptured* AVM is one discovered before any bleed, often incidentally or after a seizure or headache. For unruptured AVMs, clinicians face one of the most contested decisions in neurovascular medicine: whether to treat the malformation — through surgery, catheter-based embolisation, or focused radiation, each of which carries its own risk of causing a stroke — or to leave it in place and monitor the patient, accepting the future rupture risk.

The single major randomized trial to address this question, ARUBA, concluded that monitoring produced better outcomes than treating [1, 3]. That conclusion was, and remains, heavily disputed, because the trial was stopped early, enrolled relatively few patients, and used treatment approaches that many surgeons considered non-representative of best practice [1, 2, 5, 13]. The purpose of the present work is to revisit that question using a **target trial emulation** — a method that uses large real-world patient records to mimic a randomized trial when conducting a new one is not feasible [11, 12] — thereby re-testing "treat versus monitor" at greater scale, under modern practice, and with longer follow-up than ARUBA could achieve.

In one sentence, the request is for a cohort of patients with an unruptured brain AVM, accompanied by enough clinical detail — in particular a measure of how severe or complex each malformation is — and by sufficiently long follow-up to compare those who were treated with those who were monitored. The central challenge, addressed in detail below, is that the standard measure of AVM severity is derived from angiographic imaging and radiology reports rather than from billing or diagnosis codes. The success of this request therefore depends on what note-derived "concepts" Truveta can provide to capture severity.

## The ARUBA trial and why it remains unresolved

ARUBA was a multicentre, non-blinded randomized controlled trial conducted at 39 sites, enrolling 226 adults aged 18 or older with an unruptured brain AVM [1, 3]. Participants were randomly assigned in equal proportion either to intervention — neurosurgery, embolisation, and/or stereotactic radiosurgery, alone or in combination — or to medical management alone. The primary outcome was a composite of death from any cause or symptomatic stroke confirmed on imaging. Although follow-up was planned to run for five to ten years, the trial was halted early at a mean of roughly 33 months, at which point the intervention group had fared substantially worse (approximately 30.7% versus 10.1% reaching the primary outcome) [1]. Extended follow-up reported in 2020 continued to favour medical management [2].

The result has been criticised on several grounds. Few patients in the intervention arm underwent microsurgical resection, so the recognised gold-standard treatment was under-used and the intervention arm was heterogeneous and non-standardised [5, 13]. Because thousands of patients were screened but only 226 randomised, the trial's generalizability is limited by likely selection bias [5, 13]. The follow-up period was also short for a disease whose rupture risk accumulates over a lifetime [7, 13]. In the years after publication, practice shifted toward conservative management, and national-sample data show an increased proportion of ruptured AVM presentations (from roughly 17% to 23%) together with worse in-hospital outcomes in the post-ARUBA era [8].

## Previous attempts to revisit the question

Several observational studies have tried to re-examine ARUBA's conclusion, but each has its own limitations. A prospective, population-based Scottish cohort (SIVMS) compared conservative management with intervention and supported the conservative approach, though it is region-specific and observational [4]. Single-centre cohorts of ARUBA-eligible patients have reported good outcomes with modern microsurgery, but these are small and subject to selection bias [5]. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled interventional outcomes for ARUBA-eligible patients, but pooling observational data is not the same as a formal causal emulation [6]. A rare 30-year single-centre series has provided long-term data on conservatively managed AVMs, yet remains a single institution's experience [7]. Across all of these efforts, the recurring shortcomings are small or single-centre samples, selection bias, the absence of a formal target-trial design, and a scarcity of long-term data on conservatively managed patients.

## Why a target trial emulation adds value

A large-scale, multi-site EHR emulation can address these gaps directly. By specifying an explicit protocol, drawing on modern real-world practice, extending follow-up well beyond ARUBA's, and applying formal causal-inference methods to control confounding, such a study can test "treat versus monitor" at a scale and level of generalizability that neither ARUBA nor its observational re-analyses could reach [11, 12]. The clinical decision it informs is high-stakes and genuinely unresolved, which is what makes the effort worthwhile.

## The proposed emulation

The design follows the standard target-trial protocol, specifying eligibility, treatment strategies, treatment assignment, time zero and follow-up, the outcome, the causal contrast, and the analysis [11]. Eligibility mirrors ARUBA: adults aged 18 or older with an incident, unruptured brain AVM (ICD-10 **Q28.2**) that is considered treatable and who are functionally independent at baseline (modified Rankin Scale, mRS, below 2). Patients are excluded if they have any prior intracranial haemorrhage before the index date (ICD-10 I60–I62), have already received AVM treatment (embolisation, resection, or radiosurgery), or have a baseline mRS of 2 or greater.

The two treatment strategies are intervention — surgery, embolisation, or radiosurgery, alone or combined, initiated within a defined grace period after eligibility — and conservative management, meaning no intervention beyond symptom control. Time zero is the date of first AVM diagnosis, and the grace period and its attendant immortal-time bias are handled using a clone-censor-weight or sequential-emulation approach [11]. The primary outcome reproduces ARUBA's composite of all-cause death and symptomatic stroke (intracranial haemorrhage or infarction, ICD-10 I60–I63), which requires linkage to mortality data alongside the relevant diagnosis codes; useful secondary outcomes, where available, include functional decline, seizure control, and procedural complications.

Because rupture risk is cumulative, the emulation needs long and continuous follow-up — ideally five to ten years — with low loss to follow-up, so the median continuous enrolment achievable in the cohort is an important thing to establish. Scale is likewise central: ARUBA randomised only 226 patients from a much larger screened population, whereas an EHR cohort should yield far more, and statistical power will depend on the size of the eligible, emulable subset. Finally, and most importantly, the validity of the comparison hinges on controlling **confounding by indication** — the fact that larger and more eloquently located AVMs tend to be treated differently, and that AVM severity independently drives outcomes [4, 13]. Adjusting for severity is therefore essential, alongside standard covariates such as age, sex, mode of presentation (incidental, seizure, or headache), comorbidities, calendar year, and treating site.

## Capturing AVM severity — the central data challenge

The recognised measure of AVM severity is the **Spetzler–Martin grade**, which combines the size of the malformation's nidus (under 3 cm, 3–6 cm, or over 6 cm), whether it sits in an eloquent region of the brain, and whether it drains into deep veins, yielding a grade from I to V [9]. Every one of these components is read from angiographic or MRI imaging and recorded in radiology reports. Because Truveta holds the coded record rather than the images themselves, severity must be recovered from note-derived concepts, and the feasibility of that extraction is the pivotal question for this request. Ideally the extract would include the individual Spetzler–Martin components (nidus size, eloquence of location, and deep venous drainage), a derived Spetzler–Martin grade or Spetzler–Ponce class where available, and, if possible, supplemental factors such as nidus compactness or diffuseness from the Lawton–Young system [10].

Several severity-relevant elements are, by contrast, already available in structured data. The AVM diagnosis is captured by ICD-10 Q28.2; rupture status and stroke outcomes are captured by the haemorrhage and infarction codes I60–I63; and common presentations such as seizure are captured by G40. The treatment arms can be defined, and prior treatment washed out, using procedure codes for microsurgical resection (CPT 61680–61692, "surgery of intracranial AVM"), transcatheter embolisation (CPT 61624 and related supervision-and-interpretation codes), and stereotactic radiosurgery or Gamma Knife (CPT 61796–61800 and 77371–77373); the exact code sets should be confirmed with the team. Functional status via the modified Rankin Scale is needed for both the entry criterion and the outcomes but is often not coded, so its availability as a concept is worth confirming, as are adjuncts such as NIHSS, hydrocephalus, and any associated aneurysm.

## What we are requesting

We are asking Truveta for a cohort and data extract sufficient to support this emulation, together with guidance on what is feasible before the December 2026 access cutoff. Concretely, this means: a cohort of treatment-naïve adults with unruptured brain AVM (Q28.2) who are functionally independent at baseline, reported with patient counts at each filtering step; procedure data to define the intervention and conservative arms; all-cause mortality linkage together with haemorrhage and infarction outcomes; the longest available continuous follow-up; the AVM severity concepts described above, with an indication of their coverage, cost, and provisioning timeline; and standard baseline covariates. The overriding question we would like answered is whether enough severity signal can be obtained from concepts to control confounding by indication — the very factor at the heart of the ARUBA debate. Specifically, we would like to know which Spetzler–Martin components are available as extracted concepts and at what coverage, whether a Spetzler–Martin grade or mRS is captured anywhere, and what the cost and lead time would be to provision the relevant radiology-report concepts before access ends.
